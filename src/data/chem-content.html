01 / The method

Show the route, not just the result.

Lecture 6 repeatedly asks: What was given? What do you want? What relationship connects them? Does the answer make sense? A correct number alone does not demonstrate the process. Every change of number or unit needs visible work. [L6, lines 718–794, 885–1032]

  1. Name the entity. Grams of O₂, moles of O₂, O₂ molecules, and O atoms are different quantities.
  2. Write given → wanted. If there is no direct bridge, name the intermediate units.
  3. Choose a true equality. 1 mol O₂ = 32.00 g O₂; 1 mol = 6.022 × 10²³ entities.
  4. Orient each factor. Put the unit you have on the opposite side so it cancels. Leave the wanted unit.
  5. Calculate last. Multiply numerators, divide by denominators. Keep extra digits until the end.
  6. Explain and check. Predict larger/smaller than one mole, verify units, and state what the answer counts.
An exam-ready answer has three layers. A labeled setup with units; the evaluated answer with units; one sentence linking the result to a physical claim. Never write “more particles because it is bigger” without saying whether you mean mass, volume, molecule size, or count.
m = nMN = nNAPV = nRTρ = m/V

m: sample mass (g) · M: molar mass (g/mol) · n: amount (mol) · N: entity count · NA: 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ · R: 0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹. Use T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15. These are course-rounded constants. [M3, pp. 18–32]

02 / What are you counting?

One mole is a count. Molar mass is a bridge.

Atom, molecule, substance, phase

An element contains one type of atom. A molecule has two or more bonded atoms; O₂ is both a molecule and an elemental substance. A compound contains at least two elements chemically combined. A mixture contains independent substances in variable proportions.

Count distinct particle identities to count substances. Count separate uniform physical regions to count phases. Water liquid + water vapor is one substance in two phases; O₂ and N₂ mixed as gases are two substances in one phase.

For the course’s common elemental forms, remember H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂. A hydrogen atom inside H₂O is not a separate H₂ molecule. The formula determines the counts. [M3, pp. 3–11]

Same count, different mass

One mole He and one mole Ar each contain 6.022 × 10²³ atoms. Ar has the larger mass because each Ar atom is more massive. Equal gram amounts reverse the comparison: the smaller molar mass gives more moles and more particles.

One mole CO₂ contains one mole of molecules, one mole of C atoms, two moles of O atoms, and three moles of total atoms. The subscript multiplies the atom count, not the molecule count.

Mass is not physical diameter. In this guide “heavier” means greater mass. Do not infer atomic radius from atomic mass.

03 / Conversion workbench

Every factor earns its place.

Cancel units, not labels in your head. Change the quantity or reverse a route; the full chain updates below.

A. Grams → moles → molecules → atoms

Given: 11.5 g ethanol, C₂H₆O. Wanted: molecules, then H atoms. First calculate M = 2(12.01) + 6(1.008) + 16.00 = 46.068 ≈ 46.07 g/mol. [L6, lines 700–858]

11.5 g C₂H₆O × 1 mol C₂H₆O46.07 g C₂H₆O × 6.022 × 10²³ molecules C₂H₆O1 mol C₂H₆O = 1.50 × 10²³ molecules

Then (1.503 × 10²³ molecules C₂H₆O) × (6 H atoms / 1 molecule C₂H₆O) = 9.02 × 10²³ H atoms. The 6 comes from the formula. It is not a molar mass. The starting sample is about a quarter of 46.07 g, so about a quarter mole is reasonable.

B. Reverse the entire route

1.503 × 10²³ molecules C₂H₆O × 1 mol C₂H₆O6.022 × 10²³ molecules C₂H₆O × 46.07 g C₂H₆O1 mol C₂H₆O ≈ 11.5 g

The equalities did not change. Their orientation changed because the starting unit changed. Multiplying a molecule count by Avogadro’s number would leave the wrong units.

C. Liquid volume adds another bridge

For the lecture water example, use the stated approximation ρ = 1.00 g/mL; this is not the density of every liquid.

2.00 L × 1000 mL1 L × 1.00 g1 mL × 1 mol H₂O18.02 g × 6.022 × 10²³ molecules H₂O1 mol H₂O = 6.68 × 10²⁵ molecules

Intermediate results: 2000 mL → 2000 g → 111 mol. That is roughly 100 moles, so roughly 100NA molecules is plausible. The reverse route uses mol/molecules, g/mol, mL/g, then L/mL. [L6, lines 879–1032; M3, pp. 25–28]

D. Molecules → mass, with a diatomic trap

5.0 × 10²¹ molecules O₂ × 1 mol O₂6.022 × 10²³ molecules O₂ × 32.00 g O₂1 mol O₂ = 0.266 g O₂

If instead given 5.0 × 10²¹ O atoms contained in O₂, first divide by 2 O atoms per molecule; the resulting mass is 0.133 g. Naming the entity changes the answer by a factor of two. [L6, lines 1034–1074]

04 / Gas reasoning

Connect the count to pressure and volume.

PV = NkBT becomes PV = nRT by substituting N = nNA, with R = NAkB. Both equations count gas entities; one uses individual entities and one uses moles. [L7, opening gas-law review; M3, pp. 31–33]

ChangeHeld fixedPredictionParticle explanation
T increasesn, VP increasesFaster particles hit walls more often and transfer more momentum per collision.
n increasesT, VP increasesMore particles produce more wall collisions.
V decreasesn, TP increasesParticles reach walls more frequently; average kinetic energy stays fixed.
M increasesn, T, VP unchanged, speeds lowerLower speeds and larger mass compensate in the ideal-gas pressure relation.

Equal P, V, T → equal gas-particle counts, not necessarily atom counts

Since n = PV/RT, identical P, V, T give equal n for ideal gases. One bottle of He and one of O₂ can have equal entity counts but different masses and different total atom counts. At equal temperature they have the same average translational kinetic energy; heavier O₂ has lower characteristic speeds. Compare v ∝ √(T/M), with temperatures in kelvin. [M2, pp. 10–21; A3, Q11–20]

Worked: mass → gas volume, then reverse

Given: 3.20 g O₂ at 25.0°C and 1.00 atm. Wanted: V. M(O₂) = 32.00 g/mol; T = 298.15 K.

n = 3.20 g O₂ × (1 mol O₂ / 32.00 g O₂) = 0.100 mol O₂
V = nRT/P = (0.100 mol)(0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(298.15 K)/(1.00 atm) = 2.45 L

Reverse: n = PV/RT = 0.100 mol; m = nM = 3.20 g. The kelvin, atm, and mol units cancel appropriately; liters remain when solving for volume.

22.4 L/mol is conditional. The course uses 0°C and 1 atm as STP, giving about 22.4 L/mol for an ideal gas. Do not use it at 25°C, for a liquid, or at another pressure. Read the conditions even if a problem says “standard.”

Two changes at once

For fixed n: P₂/P₁ = (T₂/T₁)(V₁/V₂). Doubling kelvin temperature while halving volume makes pressure four times larger. Tripling temperature and doubling external pressure for an equilibrated flexible balloon gives V₂/V₁ = 3/2: a 10 L balloon becomes 15 L. Specify fixed amount and absolute temperature.

05 / Isotopes & averages

The average describes a population.

Atomic number Z is the proton count. A = protons + neutrons, so neutrons = A − Z. Isotopes keep Z and change the neutron count. Ionization changes electron count, not elemental identity: a +1 ion has Z − 1 electrons. [M4, pp. 6–10; A5, Q1–5]

Average atomic mass = Σ(fractional abundance × isotopic mass)

For chlorine: (0.7577)(34.9689 amu) + (0.2423)(36.9659 amu) = 35.4528 ≈ 35.45 amu. This must lie between the two masses and closer to the more abundant isotope. Taking the simple mean would incorrectly assume equal abundance. [A5, table 1 and Q8–14]

Reverse problem: recover abundance from an average

Original hypothetical example: an element has only two isotopes with masses 10.00 and 11.00 amu; its average is 10.80 amu. Let x be the fraction of the lighter isotope. The heavy fraction is 1 − x.

10.00x + 11.00(1 − x) = 10.80 → 11.00 − x = 10.80 → x = 0.20

Thus 20% light and 80% heavy. Two isotope masses alone cannot determine an average; you also need abundances. An average alone does not uniquely determine all isotope masses and abundances.

From an average atom to a mole

35.4528 amu/atom × 1.6606 × 10⁻²⁴ g1 amu = 5.8873 × 10⁻²³ g/atom

Then (5.8873 × 10⁻²³ g/atom)(6.022 × 10²³ atoms) ≈ 35.45 g. This explains why the numerical value in amu per average atom corresponds, at course precision, to g/mol. Do not multiply 35.45 g by NA again. [A5, Q9–16]

06 / Inside the instrument

Prepare. Charge. Accelerate. Bend. Detect.

Follow one sample through the magnetic-sector teaching model used in class. Deflection happens after ionization and acceleration; fragmentation can happen back in the ion source.

Vaporization supplies gas-phase particles that can travel independently through the instrument. It changes phase; it does not itself require breaking molecules into atoms.

  1. Vaporization: prepare gas-phase sample particles. Heating to vaporize separates particles; it is distinct from fragmentation.
  2. Ionization: in the electron-impact example, energetic electrons collide with the sample and knock out electrons, forming positive ions, often +1. The nucleus and proton count are unchanged. Enough deposited energy can also break molecular bonds.
  3. Acceleration: electric fields accelerate ions into a narrow beam. Under the activity’s equal-charge acceleration conditions, the ions have the same kinetic energy, not the same speed. From KE = ½mv², heavier ions move slower.
  4. Deflection: moving ions enter the analyzing magnetic field, which bends their paths. With equal charge and kinetic energy, smaller-mass ions have tighter curvature; larger-mass ions curve less. More generally the instrument separates by mass-to-charge ratio, m/z.
  5. Detection: selected ions hit the detector, producing an electrical signal. Scanning the field can bring different m/z ions to a fixed detector; the plotted spectrum records signal versus m/z.

Why vacuum? Collisions with background gas could scatter ions, change their paths or remove them before detection. A vacuum reduces those collisions. It does not create the ions or turn every molecule into an intact molecular ion. [A6, Model 1 and Q1–7; M4, pp. 3–7]

Clarification of the classroom schematic: electric fields accelerate positive ions toward negative electrodes. Magnetic bending acts on moving charges; a magnet is not a positive electrode attracting a negative one. The drawing is a sequence schematic, not a scale-accurate path to a detector. Different instrument designs use different analyzers. [JEOL instrument references]

Fragmentation: broken bonds, not new isotopes

An energized molecular ion may break into smaller fragments in the ion source. Some fragments remain charged and are detected; neutral fragments are not directly detected as mass peaks. One possible methane pathway is CH₄⁺ → CH₃⁺ + H·. The parent has m/z ≈ 16 and the charged fragment m/z ≈ 15 for +1 ions.

This does not mean your original sample contained bottles of pure CH₃. It means the measurement process can produce that fragment. An isotope peak near 17 can instead be intact methane containing ¹³C. Fragment loss and isotope substitution are different explanations. [L7, methane and methanol discussion; M4, pp. 14–15; A6, Q14–15]

07 / Read the evidence

A peak’s position and height answer different questions.

  1. Read the sample identity and charge assumption. Atomic element, diatomic element, or molecular compound? For z = +1, m/z numerically tracks ion mass; a 40 amu +2 ion would appear near 20, not 40.
  2. Read x: mass-to-charge ratio. Peak position indicates which ion mass/charge is present.
  3. Read y: percent abundance or relative intensity? Percent abundances for one isotope population sum to 100%; base-peak-normalized intensities generally do not.
  4. Assign peaks with evidence. Molecular ion, fragments, or heavier-isotope partners? The tallest peak need not be the intact molecule.
  5. Choose only the relevant peak family. An atomic weighted average uses atomic isotope abundances, not all fragments and molecular peaks pooled together.

On narrow screens, swipe the plot horizontally. The table below lists every peak.

Relative intensity → actual fractional abundance

For Mg isotope intensities 100, 13, 14 at m/z 24, 25, 26, the total is 127. Fractions are 100/127, 13/127, 14/127, giving 78.74%, 10.24%, 11.02%. They approximate the source’s 79%, 10%, 11%; its plotted intensities were rounded. Use the dataset actually given. [A6, Q12–13, pp. 4–5]

Estimated average = [24(100) + 25(13) + 26(14)] / 127 = 24.323 amu

This is an estimate because integer mass numbers approximate exact isotope masses and the graph intensities/reading are rounded. “The tallest peak is 100% of the sample” confuses a reference height with a population fraction.

Chlorine: two isotopes can produce five peaks

For ³⁵Cl (76%) and ³⁷Cl (24%), atomic fragments appear near 35 and 37. Intact Cl₂⁺ ions can be 35+35 = 70, 35+37 = 72, or 37+37 = 74. The mixed pair has two arrangements, so its probability is 2pq. [A6, Q15; M4, pp. 12–13]

08 / From measurements to formulas

Composition gives the ratio. Mass gives the multiplier.

Mass spectrometry helps determine molecular mass. Elemental analysis provides element mass percentages. Neither alone generally supplies a unique formula. An empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number atom ratio; a molecular formula gives actual counts per molecule. [M4, pp. 16–23]

  1. Assume 100 g when given percentages. A different sample size works too; 100 makes percent numerically equal to grams.
  2. Convert each element separately to moles: grams × (1 mol element / atomic molar mass).
  3. Divide every mole amount by the smallest. The result is a ratio, not the final molecule count.
  4. Make all ratios whole. 1:1.5 → multiply both by 2 → 2:3. Do not round 1.5 to 2. Near-integer deviations from measurement/rounding are different from simple fractions.
  5. Calculate empirical-formula mass. Add atomic molar masses using its subscripts.
  6. Find k = molecular molar mass / empirical-formula mass. It should be close to a positive integer. Multiply every empirical subscript by k.
  7. Check both constraints. Recalculate mass and percent composition. A correct mass alone is not enough.

Course example: 92.26% C, 7.74% H; M = 78.1 g/mol

C: 92.26 g × (1 mol C / 12.01 g C) = 7.682 mol C
H: 7.74 g × (1 mol H / 1.008 g H) = 7.679 mol H
Divide by 7.679 → C:H ≈ 1:1 → empirical formula CH
M(CH) = 12.01 + 1.008 = 13.018 g/mol
k = 78.1 / 13.018 ≈ 6 → molecular formula C₆H₆

The 7.68 mol values refer to your assumed 100 g bulk sample. They are not fractional atom subscripts. Dividing percentages directly would ignore the different masses of C and H. [L7, closing empirical-formula example; M4, pp. 17–18]

Course example: equal masses do not mean equal atom counts

50.0 g S × (1 mol S / 32.07 g S) = 1.559 mol S
50.0 g O × (1 mol O / 16.00 g O) = 3.125 mol O
S:O ≈ 1:2 → SO₂; empirical mass ≈ 64.07 g/mol
Given M ≈ 64 g/mol → k ≈ 1 → molecular formula SO₂

O atoms are about half as massive as S atoms, so equal masses require about twice as many O atoms. [M4, pp. 19–20]

Course example: the fraction must be cleared

Assume 100 g: 29.31 g C, 1.476 g H, 69.22 g Cl
C: 29.31/12.01 = 2.440 mol; H: 1.476/1.008 = 1.464 mol; Cl: 69.22/35.45 = 1.953 mol
Divide by 1.464 → 1.667 : 1 : 1.333

M(empirical) ≈ 204.87 g/mol; 410/204.87 ≈ 2 → C₁₀H₆Cl₈

Check: 10(12.01) + 6(1.008) + 8(35.45) ≈ 409.75 g/mol. This reconciles the given 410 g/mol within rounding. [M4, pp. 21–22]

Course closing prompt: 57.11% C, 9.586% H, 33.29% N

Moles in 100 g: 57.11/12.01 = 4.755 mol C; 9.586/1.008 = 9.510 mol H; 33.29/14.01 = 2.376 mol N. Ratio ≈ 2:4:1 gives C₂H₄N, empirical mass ≈ 42.06 g/mol. The supplied spectrum’s upper-mass cluster is near 84; taking its molecular ion near 84 gives k ≈ 2 and C₄H₈N₂. This mass is a visual estimate from the slide, not a precisely labeled value; use the peak designated in an exam. [M4, p. 23]

Combustion analysis: what can and cannot be inferred?

For complete combustion of a C/H/O compound, moles C in the original sample equal moles CO₂ collected; moles H atoms equal twice the moles H₂O collected. Oxygen in CO₂ and H₂O can come from the added O₂ as well as the sample, so do not assign all product oxygen to the original compound. If the original sample is known to contain only C, H, O, calculate its O mass by subtracting the recovered C and H masses from the original sample mass. 09 / Supporting ideas

Keep the old concepts available.

Forces and potential energy

With PE = 0 at infinite separation, attractive pairs have negative PE near their stable separation. Pulling them far apart raises PE toward zero. Pushing them too close produces strong repulsion and raises PE. At the minimum, attractive and repulsive forces balance; the net force is zero, not each individual force.

A deeper well means more energy is needed to separate the pair. Well depth is not identical to force at every distance; force relates to the curve’s slope. Small displacement from the minimum produces a restoring tendency.

Temperature and phase changes

On a constant-pressure cooling curve for a pure substance, temperature slopes downward within a phase and remains flat while a phase transition proceeds at equilibrium. Average kinetic energy tracks temperature. During condensation at a fixed-temperature plateau, average KE stays constant and PE decreases as particles come together.

Higher boiling temperature at a common pressure generally signals stronger attractions in the course comparison. Higher vapor pressure at a common temperature means greater volatility. Boiling occurs when vapor pressure equals external pressure.

These explain background reasoning in Learning Check 1; they are supporting review here, not a claim that the same questions recur. [A4, Q5–15; M2, pp. 23–33; M1, pp. 33–47]

Source wording to handle carefully

Activity 4 Q4 invites a general claim that KE increases from solid to liquid to gas. State the temperature conditions: at the same-temperature phase-change plateau, the average KE does not rise. Activity 3 Q19 says to vary mass but repeats volume labels; its intended comparison is pressure versus particle mass at fixed T, N, V. M3 p. 16 mixes 1.008 g and 1.00 g H and asks about “atoms” of water; count total atoms explicitly rather than silently substituting molecules. Some non-diatomic elements form extended solids or other molecular forms; the seven-element list is a common diatomic mnemonic, not a universal description of all elemental structures.

Q3 · Isotopes and deflection

d. The 10 ion bends more tightly, specifically in the analyzing magnetic-field region after acceleration, under the stated equal-KE, equal-charge conditions.

e. 0.500 mol × (10.80 g/mol) = 5.40 g.

f. Neither isotope has mass 10.80 amu in this model; it is a weighted mean. The 100 intensity is relative to the largest peak, not the sum.

Q4 · Atomic and molecular families

a. 35 = ³⁵Cl⁺; 37 = ³⁷Cl⁺; 70 = ³⁵Cl–³⁵Cl⁺; 72 = mixed pair; 74 = ³⁷Cl–³⁷Cl⁺. Two isotopes produce atomic fragments plus molecular combinations.

b. Molecular probabilities: 0.76² : 2(0.76)(0.24) : 0.24² = 57.76 : 36.48 : 5.76. Normalized to the 70 peak: 100 : 63.16 : 9.97.

c. Atomic average ≈ (0.76)(35) + (0.24)(37) = 35.48 amu. Use the two atomic isotope peaks and their normalized abundances. Exact isotope masses would give a slightly different result.

d. Energetic ionization can leave molecular ions with enough energy to dissociate in the source. Only charged products are sorted/detected in this ion measurement; neutral products are not directly recorded as ion peaks.

e. No. Isotope pairing predicts ratios within the molecular family. Atomic-to-molecular signal ratios also depend on fragmentation and detection response.

Q5 · Percentages → formula → H atoms

a. Assume 100 g.

40.00 g C × (1 mol C / 12.01 g C) = 3.331 mol C
6.71 g H × (1 mol H / 1.008 g H) = 6.657 mol H
53.29 g O × (1 mol O / 16.00 g O) = 3.331 mol O
Divide by ≈3.331 → 1:2:1 → CH₂O

b. M(CH₂O) = 12.01 + 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 30.026 g/mol; k = 180.16/30.026 ≈ 6 → C₆H₁₂O₆.

c. 1.8016 g × (1 mol / 180.16 g) × (6.022 × 10²³ molecules / mol) = 6.022 × 10²¹ molecules
Then × (12 H atoms / molecule) = 7.2264 × 10²² H atoms

d. Mass percentages are not count percentages. Different elements contribute different mass per atom; converting to moles puts their counts on a common scale.

Q6 · Complete integrated solution

a. Assume 100 g: C = 52.14/12.01 = 4.341 mol; H = 13.13/1.008 = 13.026 mol; O = 34.73/16.00 = 2.171 mol. Divide all by 2.171 → about 2:6:1. Empirical C₂H₆O; empirical mass 46.068 g/mol. Molecular mass 46.07 g/mol gives k ≈ 1, so molecular formula C₂H₆O. The formula alone does not prove a particular isomer.

b. 0.500 mL × (0.789 g / 1 mL) = 0.3945 g
× (1 mol / 46.07 g) = 0.008563 mol
× (6.022 × 10²³ molecules / 1 mol) = 5.16 × 10²¹ molecules
× (6 H atoms / 1 molecule) = 3.09 × 10²² H atoms

Cancel mL, then g, then mol, then molecules. These are consecutive factors in one chain, shown on separate lines for readability.

c. 5.1567 × 10²¹ molecules × (1 mol / 6.022 × 10²³ molecules) × (46.07 g / 1 mol) × (1 mL / 0.789 g) ≈ 0.500 mL

If you start instead from the H-atom count, insert (1 molecule / 6 H atoms) first.

d. P = nRT/V = (0.008563 mol)(0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(300 K)/(0.500 L) = 0.422 atm

The liquid volume was 0.500 mL; the gas container is 0.500 L. Gas density depends on the new P, T and phase. The liquid density is not preserved upon vaporization.

e. The peak at 31 is a fragment and happens to be the base peak; its height does not identify it as the whole molecule. A small peak near 47 is consistent with a heavier-isotope molecular ion, not proof that H was added. The explicitly labeled parent is near m/z 46; 46.07 g/mol is the independently supplied average molar mass.

f. Vaporize → ionize/possible fragment → accelerate → magnetically deflect → detect. Ionization changes charge; the analyzing field separates by m/z. Fragmentation is not the bending step.

Wrong turn: do not use 22.4 L/mol at 300 K or use the 0.789 g/mL liquid density for the gas.

Activity 3 · Q1–20 · gas behavior

Q1–5: negligible particle size relative to separations, negligible ideal-gas interactions, elastic collisions, and arrow directions/speeds. “Elastic” conserves total collision KE; it need not preserve each individual particle’s KE.

Q6–8: 15 in × (1 ft/12 in) = 1.25 ft; 1.5 ft × (12 in/1 ft) = 18 in. Orient each equality to cancel the starting unit.

Q9–10: 101.3 kPa × (1 atm/101.325 kPa) ≈ 1.000 atm; 615 mmHg × (1 atm/760 mmHg) = 0.809 atm.

Q11–14: no particles added, fixed volume, longer arrows mean higher speeds/temperature. P versus T in kelvin is a straight proportional line at fixed N,V.

Q15–17: fixed volume and temperature; same speed distribution while N increases. P versus N is proportional at fixed T,V.

Q18: collect your simulation data at fixed N,T; expect P ∝ 1/V, a decreasing hyperbola. Q19: intended P-versus-mass experiment at fixed N,T,V; expect a horizontal line. The worksheet repeats volume labels in this item.

Q20a–d: pressure direct with T; direct with N; inverse with V; independent of particle mass under ideal assumptions. Always name the variables held fixed. Review gas reasoning ↑

Activity 4 · Q1–15 · interactions

Q1–4: the conventional model shows close solid/liquid particles and widely separated gas particles; solids and liquids have definite volume; gas particles explore the whole container. Do not claim KE must change solely because the phase label changes—state temperature conditions.

Q5–6: far apart PE approaches zero; approaching from large separation lowers PE until the minimum. Q7–9: attractions become negligible at very large distance; they strengthen on approach over the relevant range, but sufficiently close particles have dominant repulsion.

Q10–11: identities affect interaction strength and the depth/shape of the PE well. Q12: attractive and repulsive forces balance at the minimum. Q13: a slightly separated pair feels a restoring tendency; without damping it may oscillate rather than simply stop at the minimum.

Q14–15: stronger attraction produces a deeper well; on shared axes draw B’s minimum more negative than A’s. This does not mean net force is greatest at the minimum. Review energy reasoning ↑

Activity 5 · Q1–19 · isotope masses and the mole

Q1–3: three listed natural Mg isotopes; same protons, different neutrons; a randomly selected C atom is most likely ¹²C, mass 12.0000 amu.

Q4–5: 1000(12.0000) = 12,000.0 amu; 1000(13.0034) = 13,003.4 amu. A random 1000-atom sample is most likely slightly above 12,000 amu because some atoms are ¹³C; individual draws fluctuate.

Q6–7: none of the four marbles weighs 6.50 g. Use fractional weights when total number is unknown: 0.372(10.0) + 0.628(12.00) = 11.256 g.

Q8–10: mean Cl = 35.4528 amu; none of the two listed isotope masses equals that mean. Mean g/atom = 35.4528(1.6606 × 10⁻²⁴) = 5.8873 × 10⁻²³. Multiplying by 6.022 × 10²³ atoms gives ≈35.45 g.

Q11–12: mean Mg = 0.7899(23.9850) + 0.1000(24.9858) + 0.1101(25.9826) = 24.305 amu; ≈4.036 × 10⁻²³ g/atom; ≈24.31 g per mole.

Q13–16: periodic-table value is the sample’s average atomic mass numerically in amu and, at course precision, molar mass in g/mol. It is not the mass of an individual average-sized isotope. In the listed C isotope model, 0% have exactly 12.0107 amu.

Q17a–f: a dozen = 12; a mole = 6.022 × 10²³; equal dozens/moles have equal counts regardless of entity identity. Q18: 24 elephants outweigh 18; a mole of Na has more atoms/mass than 5.136 × 10²³ Na atoms.

Q19: read “hydrogen atoms” and “argon atoms” as in the question sequence: 1.008 g H atoms and 39.95 g Ar atoms are each one mole of atoms. If hydrogen instead means elemental H₂ molecules, 1.008 g is half a mole of H₂ molecules but still one mole of H atoms. Review isotope reasoning ↑

Activity 6 · Q1–15 · mass spectrometry

Q1–3: the four labeled processes are ionization → acceleration → deflection → detection (vaporization prepares the sample). Match descriptions: metal plate = detection; separation = deflection; knock out electrons = ionization; charged plates = acceleration.

Q4–5: +1 ions result from electron removal; the + symbols and attraction to negative acceleration electrodes support their charge. Separation depends on m/z.

Q6–7: for equal +1 charge and equal KE, deflection ranks ¹⁶O⁺ > ¹⁷O⁺ > ¹⁹F⁺. Vacuum reduces unwanted collisions.

Q8–9: most common Mg isotope is 24, about 79%; integer-mass estimate = 24(0.79)+25(0.10)+26(0.11) = 24.32 amu. Graph rounding and approximate integer isotope masses limit precision.

Q10: among the supplied choices, C best matches a periodic-table average near 126.9, because the peak near 127 dominates. That is a comparison among offered sketches, not proof of an appreciable naturally occurring ¹²⁶I fraction.

Q11: Pb peaks: 204 at 1.4%; 206 at 24.1%; 207 at 22.1%; 208 at 52.4%. Q12–13: percent abundance partitions the population; relative intensity references the largest peak. With heights 100,13,14, sum =127 relative count units; 100/127=78.74%, 13/127=10.24%. Rounded heights cannot reproduce exact 79%/10%/11% values.

Q14: one isotope of fluorine can give F⁺ near 19 and intact F₂⁺ near 38. Q15a–c: Cl atomic peaks 35/37 and molecular peaks 70/72/74; molecular ordering 70 > 72 > 74 under random pairing. Relative atomic-to-molecular heights need fragmentation information. Review spectra ↑
