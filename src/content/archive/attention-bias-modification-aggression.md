> Student research proposal produced at Southwestern College in May 2026. The study was not conducted. This web edition removes course-administration material and adds an editorial limitations note; it is not a preregistered or implementation-ready protocol.

## Introduction

Attention-bias modification (ABM) is a computerized training approach intended to shift attention away from hostile cues. This proposal asks whether a brief ABM program would merit testing among justice-impacted young adults living in the community, with hostile attention bias and aggression scores as the primary outcomes and decision-making as an exploratory measure.

The rationale draws on three studies involving specialized, all-male offender samples. Each examines a different part of the question: attention training, a group-level neurochemical difference, or decision-making after neuromodulation. Together, they provide a basis for further study—not evidence that ABM would work in a community reentry population.

### Evidence base

Zhao et al. (2022) provides the closest intervention model. The researchers randomly assigned 84 institutionalized male young offenders between the ages of 16 and 18 to an ABM group, a placebo group, or a waiting-list group. Before and after treatment, they measured aggression, attention toward positive stimuli, and attention toward hostile stimuli using the antisocial personality disorder subscale of the PDQ-4, the Aggression Questionnaire, an emotional dot-probe task, and a hostile emotional Stroop task. The authors reported that the effects of ABM varied with participants’ initial attentional patterns: those entering with higher hostile attention bias or lower positive attention bias appeared to benefit most (Zhao et al., 2022). Of the three studies, this one most directly informs the proposed intervention and outcomes.

Tully et al. (2024) approached violent behavior from a biological perspective. The researchers compared 30 violent male offenders with antisocial personality disorder, with and without psychopathy, with 21 healthy male non-offenders. Using proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy, they found a significantly reduced striatal glutamate:GABA ratio in the offender group relative to the non-offender group. This was a group-level neurochemical difference in a small all-male sample. It does not establish that the ratio caused violent behavior, that it represents a general behavioral-regulation deficit, or that the same difference would appear in the population proposed here.

Kuhn et al. (2024) studied decision-making in 22 male violent offenders and 24 male non-violent controls. Participants completed active and sham transcranial direct current stimulation over the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, followed by the Balloon Analogue Risk Task during fMRI scanning. The authors reported less optimal decision-making in the offender group and improved decision-making with increased prefrontal activity following active stimulation in that group (Kuhn et al., 2024). This study does not provide evidence about ABM, but it demonstrates one way decision-making can be measured as a secondary behavioral outcome.

Taken together, the studies support a narrow rationale for testing whether attention training might affect hostile attention bias and aggression. Zhao et al. (2022) supplies the direct intervention model. Tully et al. (2024) and Kuhn et al. (2024) provide background on other processes studied in small offender samples, but neither shows that ABM would work for community reentry participants. Brain imaging and tDCS are therefore outside the scope of this proposal.

### Proposed question and hypotheses

The primary hypothesis is that participants assigned to ABM would show greater pretest-to-posttest reductions in hostile attention bias and aggression scores than participants assigned to neutral attention training. Any difference on the decision-making task would be treated as exploratory rather than as a primary test of the intervention.

## Methods

### Participants

The proposed sample would include approximately 48 justice-impacted young adults between the ages of 18 and 25. For this study, *justice-impacted* would refer to people who had previously been incarcerated in juvenile detention, jail, or prison and were living in the community during reentry.

Recruitment would take place through community reentry organizations, peer-support groups, and community college programs serving formerly incarcerated students. Participants would need to be at least 18 years old, able to read English, and currently out of custody. They would be excluded if they reported an active psychotic disorder, major cognitive impairment, or acute intoxication at the time of testing because those conditions could interfere with task completion or informed consent. Participation would be voluntary.

### Design

The study would use a randomized, between-groups pretest-posttest design. Participants would be assigned to either an ABM condition or a neutral attention-training control condition. Training condition would be the independent variable. Hostile attention bias and aggression scores would be the primary dependent variables, measured before and after training. A brief computerized decision-making task would provide a secondary exploratory outcome.

This structure follows the randomized intervention model used by Zhao et al. (2022) while adapting the proposed setting and population.

### Materials

Participants would complete a demographic questionnaire covering age, gender, race or ethnicity, time since release, type of correctional setting, and current school or employment status.

Hostile attention bias would be measured with a brief hostile emotional Stroop task modeled after Zhao et al. (2022). Participants would identify the display color of hostile and neutral words as quickly as possible. The proposed bias score would subtract reaction times for neutral words from reaction times for hostile words. A higher positive score would indicate greater interference from hostile material.

An aggression questionnaire would assess physical aggression, verbal aggression, anger, and hostility. Zhao et al. (2022) reported strong internal consistency for the aggression measure used in its young-offender sample, but that result does not validate an unspecified instrument for this proposed population. A final protocol would need to select, name, and justify a validated measure.

Participants would also complete a brief computerized decision-making task based on the type of risk-and-reward task used by Kuhn et al. (2024). This measure would remain exploratory; hostile attention bias and aggression would be the study’s primary outcomes.

### Procedure

Eligible participants would attend an in-person pretest session. After reviewing and signing an informed-consent form, they would complete the demographic questionnaire, hostile emotional Stroop task, aggression questionnaire, and decision-making task.

Participants would then be randomly assigned to ABM or neutral attention training. The ABM condition would use a visual-search program designed to direct attention toward positive targets and away from hostile distractors. The control condition would use a task matched for structure and duration without training attention away from hostile content.

Participants would complete four training sessions across two weeks, with each session lasting approximately 15 to 20 minutes. After the final session, they would repeat the baseline measures. The intended comparison would examine whether pretest-to-posttest change differed between conditions, although the inferential model and missing-data procedure have not yet been specified. Participants would then be debriefed, thanked, and given a small gift card.

### Ethics and confidentiality

The study could proceed only after institutional review board approval. Because participants would have histories of incarceration, the consent and recruitment processes would require particular care. Participation would not be connected to probation, parole, court status, housing, treatment access, or school standing. Recruitment would not occur through correctional officers or anyone with direct authority over prospective participants. Compensation would require review for undue influence, and participants would be told that they could stop at any time without penalty.

Each participant would receive an ID number. The identity key would be stored separately from the study data with restricted access, defined retention limits, and a deletion schedule approved before recruitment. Results would be reported only at the group level.

The study would not use deception. After participation, each person would receive a written debriefing and a list of counseling and reentry-support resources in case the study topics caused discomfort.

## Discussion

Because the study was not conducted, the expected pattern is a hypothesis rather than a finding. The proposal anticipates greater reductions in hostile attention bias and aggression in the ABM condition than in the neutral-training condition. Any improvement on the decision-making task would be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory result.

If those hypotheses were supported, the findings would justify further research into attention processes in community reentry settings; they would not establish a ready-to-use intervention. Aggression would still be measured partly through self-report, the training period would be brief, and the design includes no longer-term follow-up. A community reentry sample would also differ substantially from the institutionalized youth studied by Zhao et al. (2022), so results could not be assumed to generalize across settings or populations.

## Editorial limitations note

This classroom proposal does not include a statistical power analysis, a fully specified validated aggression instrument, a primary analysis and missing-data plan, or operational screening procedures. Those elements—and formal ethics review—would be required before the study could be implemented. The proposed sample of 48 is therefore illustrative, not justified as sufficient to detect an effect.
