Yes. As a fictional reinterpretation, you can make a character who is recognizably Kropotkin on the surface while changing the economic engine underneath him. The trick is not to make him secretly love corporations, landlords, or concentrated capital. That would immediately break the disguise. Instead, reinterpret “free-market capitalism” as the mechanism he reluctantly concludes best produces the outcomes he already values.

Historically, Kropotkin was an anarchist communist who emphasized mutual aid, decentralization, voluntary cooperation, equality, and opposition to centralized authority and monopolistic concentrations of power. So this would be a deliberate counterfactual, not a claim about what he actually believed. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][1])

The core move would be this:

Surface Kropotkin:
“Human beings flourish through voluntary cooperation rather than coercive hierarchy.”

Hidden market Kropotkin:
“And a genuinely free market is simply voluntary cooperation scaled through prices, contracts, exchange, entrepreneurship, and decentralized information.”

That gives you a surprisingly coherent inversion.

I would build him around these ten principles.

1. Mutual aid becomes the foundation of markets.

Do not have him say competition is the essence of capitalism.

Have him argue the opposite.

Competition exists, but civilization is primarily built through cooperation. Markets succeed precisely because millions of strangers voluntarily cooperate without a central commander.

A loaf of bread embodies farmers, millers, engineers, truck drivers, merchants, financiers, mechanics, and thousands of indirect contributors.

His argument becomes:

“The market is not the war of each against all. Properly understood, it is cooperation among strangers.”

That sounds extremely Kropotkinian while quietly moving toward Hayek.

2. Attack capitalism as it exists while defending markets.

This is essential.

Your character should still absolutely despise:

corporate subsidies,
monopolies,
patents used as rent extraction,
bank bailouts,
regulatory capture,
land speculation,
oligarchy,
company towns,
political corruption,
inherited privilege,
state-created barriers to entry.

He calls this “capitalism.”

But underneath, his proposed solution is:

remove privileges,
remove subsidies,
remove monopoly protections,
remove licensing barriers,
remove protectionism,
remove political favoritism,
allow unrestricted voluntary exchange.

Eventually the reader realizes:

Wait.

This dude's proposed economic system is basically an extremely radical free market.

That is much more interesting than simply making him a closet capitalist.

3. Separate capital from capitalist privilege.

He could argue:

There is nothing inherently oppressive about someone owning a machine.

The oppression occurs when institutions give the owner coercive privileges over others.

So:

Private workshop? Fine.

Privately owned factory? Potentially fine.

Hiring workers? Fine if genuinely voluntary.

Accumulating wealth? Fine.

Investing capital? Fine.

Profit? Fine.

Interest? Fine.

Rent? Potentially fine.

But state-created monopoly rents or coercively protected privilege?

Absolutely not.

This lets him retain his attack on “the capitalist class” while quietly redefining the actual objection.

4. Turn decentralization into market price theory.

This could be one of your strongest intellectual moves.

Kropotkin already distrusts centralized organization.

So your fictional Kropotkin encounters the economic calculation problem independently.

He asks:

How could a planning committee possibly know:

how much copper society should mine,
whether copper should become wire or pipes,
whether wheat should become bread or alcohol,
whether engineers should build bridges or generators,
whether scarce land should become farms or housing?

His conclusion:

Nobody can know centrally.

Information is distributed.

Prices become a communication system.

That produces something like:

“An economy must think locally.”

That feels very compatible with his philosophy.

And suddenly you've smuggled Hayek into Kropotkin.

5. Make entrepreneurship a form of mutual aid.

This is where the character gets really interesting.

An entrepreneur notices:

People need something.

I know how to produce it.

They voluntarily give me resources because what I created improves their lives.

Your Kropotkin therefore reframes entrepreneurship as:

problem recognition + voluntary cooperation + experimentation.

Profit becomes feedback.

Not moral virtue.

Just information.

Profit says:

“You transformed resources into something people valued more.”

Loss says:

“You consumed resources people valued more elsewhere.”

Now you've turned the profit-and-loss system into a decentralized ecological feedback mechanism.

A geographer/naturalist Kropotkin could find that extremely compelling.

6. Make markets ecological.

This might be your coolest philosophical bridge.

Think ecosystems.

No central wolf commands the forest.

No ministry assigns nutrients.

No tree possesses a master plan for the biome.

Millions of localized interactions produce large-scale order.

Your fictional Kropotkin sees economic coordination similarly.

Not:

“Nature is competition.”

That would contradict his Mutual Aid argument.

Instead:

“Nature consists of cooperation and competition interacting inside decentralized adaptive systems.”

Therefore human economic systems should resemble ecosystems:

distributed,
adaptive,
redundant,
bottom-up,
self-correcting,
diverse.

Markets become economic ecosystems.

Central planning becomes monoculture.

That is very Kropotkin-looking from twenty feet away.

7. Let him remain radically pro-cooperative.

Do not eliminate communes, cooperatives, mutual societies, worker-owned firms, gift economies, or collective ownership.

Make him aggressively defend all of them.

But add one principle:

Nobody gets to mandate the organizational form.

Therefore a free society contains:

worker cooperatives,
sole proprietorships,
communes,
partnerships,
corporations,
mutual-aid societies,
nonprofits,
independent craftsmen,
private firms,
gift networks.

And he says:

“If cooperation truly surpasses wage labor, it requires no policeman to prove it.”

That is the ideological trapdoor.

He looks anarcho-communist.

But he has just defended competitive institutional pluralism.

8. Make voluntary association absolutely sacred.

This is the philosophical fulcrum.

Suppose ten people voluntarily create a commune.

Fine.

Suppose ten people voluntarily create a cooperative.

Fine.

Suppose ten people voluntarily create a company where one supplies capital and nine work for wages.

Your Kropotkin now faces a problem.

If he prohibits arrangement #3, he needs authority to stop consenting adults from making it.

Therefore:

Either voluntary association is actually voluntary,

or somebody must police which voluntary relationships are ideologically permissible.

He chooses voluntary association.

That single move pulls the whole philosophy toward markets.

9. Make equality anti-privilege rather than equality-of-outcome.

He would still talk constantly about equality.

But define equality as:

equal legal standing,
absence of inherited political privilege,
absence of monopoly privilege,
freedom of association,
freedom of movement,
access to institutions,
freedom from domination.

He might actually hate enormous inequality because he believes it usually signals privilege or monopoly.

But he refuses to impose numerical economic equality because doing so requires centralized coercion.

So:

“Equality before one another, not equality measured by the accountant.”

Now you're basically constructing left-libertarian market anarchism.

10. Make abundance destroy communism's necessity.

This is the biggest inversion.

Kropotkin cared enormously about productive abundance.

Your fictional version observes that industrial productivity keeps increasing.

But instead of concluding:

abundance → communism,

he concludes:

abundance → markets make basic goods so cheap that coercive redistribution becomes increasingly unnecessary.

Capital accumulation → machines.

Machines → productivity.

Productivity → falling real costs.

Falling costs → widespread abundance.

Abundance → decreased material domination.

Therefore capital itself gradually undermines scarcity.

He could still imagine a quasi-communist endpoint.

But hilariously, capitalism becomes the mechanism that creates it.

That gives you an excellent ideological paradox:

He wants something resembling communism as a social condition while believing radical markets are the best mechanism for reaching it.

And I would make his vocabulary deliberately unchanged.

He does not say:

“capitalist.”

He says:

“producer.”

Not:

“private enterprise.”

But:

“free association.”

Not:

“consumer sovereignty.”

But:

“the sovereignty of human needs.”

Not:

“competition.”

But:

“experimentation.”

Not:

“price discovery.”

But:

“distributed knowledge.”

Not:

“entrepreneurship.”

But:

“initiative.”

Not:

“creative destruction.”

But:

“continuous adaptation.”

Not:

“capital allocation.”

But:

“placing resources where human beings find them useful.”

Not:

“market equilibrium.”

But:

“spontaneous coordination.”

Not:

“laissez-faire.”

But:

“society without economic command.”

So a paragraph from him might sound like:

> No ministry can know the thousand wants of a neighborhood, nor can a committee determine beforehand which workshop, crop, instrument, or method shall best answer them. Knowledge exists among the people themselves, dispersed among millions of hands and minds. Let them associate, exchange, experiment, succeed, fail, imitate, and begin again. Order need not descend from authority. It can emerge from free agreement.

On first reading: extremely anarchist.

On second reading:

Holy shit, that's a market-process argument.

That is where I would take the character.

And if you want to push the idea even further, don't make him “secretly capitalist” in the sense that he knows he's hiding it.

Make him sincerely believe he has discovered the purest form of anarchist communism.

Everyone around him calls it communism.

He calls it communism.

His books call it communism.

But when you translate his institutional prescriptions literally, you get:

private ownership,
voluntary exchange,
free prices,
entrepreneurship,
investment,
profit and loss,
no central planning,
no compulsory collectivization,
no restrictions on voluntary wage labor,
radical freedom of association,
strong anti-monopoly doctrine,
minimal or nonexistent state intervention.

At that point, the joke is philosophical rather than cosmetic:

Your “Kropotkin” has reasoned himself into anarcho-capitalism without ever changing his moral vocabulary.

The especially interesting version would not map perfectly onto Rothbard. It would be a weird third thing: economically very free-market, culturally communal, violently anti-oligarchic, suspicious of concentrated wealth, enthusiastic about worker cooperatives, committed to mutual aid, and completely unwilling to prohibit capitalist arrangements between consenting people.

Call it something like “market communism,” “anarchism of free association,” or, more provocatively, “Kropotkinian laissez-faire.”

That could be a legitimately interesting invented political philosophy rather than just Kropotkin wearing an Adam Smith mask.
