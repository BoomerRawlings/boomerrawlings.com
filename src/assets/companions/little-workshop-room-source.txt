Use one square room that functions as a workshop, study, kitchen, and living space at the same time.

Not four rooms. One room with four soft zones.

I would make it roughly 12×12 character-widths, with enough open floor space that their 8-way movement matters but they never feel lost.

The room:

Top-left: shared workbench.
Top-right: bookshelf / computer / little machine station.
Bottom-left: plants / window / small garden shelf.
Bottom-right: stove / kettle / table / food storage.
Center: large open rug with one small communal table.

That is enough.

The key is that the room should not be architecturally complicated. Its depth should come from what happens to it.

A rough layout:

```text
┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                     │
│  BOOKS / AI          WORKBENCH      │
│  ┌───────┐          ┌─────────┐     │
│  │       │          │         │     │
│  └───────┘          └─────────┘     │
│                                     │
│                                     │
│           ┌───────────┐             │
│           │   TABLE   │             │
│           └───────────┘             │
│                                     │
│                                     │
│  PLANTS / WINDOW        KITCHEN     │
│  ♧ ♧ ♧                  ▤  ◯        │
│                                     │
└─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The center should remain mostly empty.

That matters because the characters need somewhere to:

walk around each other,
carry objects,
meet,
argue,
sit,
build temporary things,
drag furniture,
inspect new objects,
wander aimlessly.

The room should feel like a stage.

The furniture around the edges creates destinations. The open middle creates behavior.

I would actually give the room only seven permanent anchors at first:

1. Workbench
2. Bookshelf
3. Computer
4. Window
5. Plant shelf
6. Stove
7. Central table

Everything else enters through play.

That gives you enormous development depth without expanding the map.

For example, the workbench begins as:

wooden table + hammer.

Later it might contain:

vise,
soldering iron,
tools,
printer,
robot arm,
fabricator,
AI-assisted design terminal.

Same physical location.

The player watches it evolve.

The bookshelf starts with maybe three books.

Later:

books,
notes,
papers,
filing system,
terminal,
AI index,
automated retrieval mechanism.

Eventually the “bookshelf” might essentially become a knowledge machine.

The plants start as two pots.

Later:

herbs,
food,
irrigation,
grow lights,
sensors,
automated gardening,
seed storage,
weird experimental plants.

The kitchen starts as:

stove + kettle.

Later:

automatic kettle,
food storage,
preserving equipment,
little cooking robot,
shared food production.

You get technological progression without adding territory.

That is exactly what you want.

The room can also physically reveal the two philosophies.

Do not permanently divide it into “his side” and “his side.”

Instead, let their behavior gradually create spatial tendencies.

Your Kropotkin character gravitates toward:

the central table,
plants,
food,
shared storage,
connected systems.

Objects he modifies tend to migrate inward.

Your Bakunin character gravitates toward:

the workbench,
window,
tools,
independent devices,
small personal systems.

Objects he modifies tend to spread outward.

That gives you a beautiful visual consequence:

Kropotkin causes networks.

Bakunin causes nodes.

One character slowly connects the room.

The other slowly distributes it.

You can literally communicate ideology through cables.

Kropotkin might eventually connect:

computer → plants → lights → kitchen → battery.

A visible little network begins appearing around the room.

Bakunin sees it and starts creating:

plant controller,
independent lamp battery,
separate kettle controller,
local computer.

Now the room itself becomes an argument.

That is much better than dialogue saying:

“Centralization versus decentralization.”

The furniture is debating.

For 8-way movement specifically, I would use a three-quarter top-down perspective rather than pure side view.

Something like classic Pokémon, old Zelda interiors, or a tiny RPG room.

This gives you:

north,
south,
east,
west,
four diagonals,

without needing complicated navigation.

Characters can:

walk behind the table,
stand beside one another,
approach furniture from different angles,
carry things diagonally,
circle an object,
pace during conversations.

That alone will make them feel much more alive.

I would also resist chairs initially.

Give them floor cushions or stools that characters can move themselves.

Movable small objects will make 8-way movement much more interesting than static décor.

The characters should constantly interact physically with the room.

Examples:

Kropotkin carries a book to the table.

Bakunin takes it back to the shelf.

Kropotkin waters plants.

Bakunin examines the watering mechanism.

Bakunin leaves a wrench on the floor.

Kropotkin puts it away.

Bakunin gets annoyed and retrieves it.

Kropotkin makes tea.

Bakunin walks over when he smells it.

Neither one needs player input for every movement.

Tiny autonomous behaviors make the room feel inhabited.

The central table should probably be the most important object in the game.

It has several functions:

new objects appear there,
the player places objects there,
the characters inspect things together,
arguments happen there,
experiments happen there,
meals happen there,
objects can remain unfinished there.

It is essentially the game's altar.

Every interesting object enters through the table.

That gives the player an immediate visual question whenever they open the game:

“What is on the table?”

I would make the window the second most important location.

Not because the player goes outside.

They never do.

The outside world exists almost entirely through the window.

Weather changes.

Light changes.

Occasionally something passes outside.

Technology changes what can be seen.

Maybe eventually:

antenna,
telescope,
weather sensor,
solar panel,
receiver.

The window gives you the feeling that the room belongs to a larger world without having to build that world.

You can get absurd mileage out of it.

Early:

rain.

Later:

a bird.

Later:

a strange drone.

Later:

distant construction.

Later:

satellite streak.

The world develops outside without becoming playable.

That maintains your scope.

I would also make the walls useful.

Do not just decorate them.

The characters gradually put things on them:

drawings,
plans,
charts,
photographs,
notes,
maps,
failed experiments,
little ideological jokes.

After twenty hours, the walls become a history of the relationship.

That creates the persistence effect cheaply.

You could have a tiny rule:

Nothing important disappears.

If an object becomes obsolete, somebody:

stores it,
hangs it,
repurposes it,
takes it apart,
leaves part of it somewhere.

The original kettle might still exist fifty hours later.

It has just become:

old kettle → automatic kettle → disconnected antique → flower pot.

Now you have history.

Another strong mechanic:

Give each character exactly one tiny personal space.

Not a bedroom.

Maybe just:

Kropotkin gets a chair/cushion near the plants.

Bakunin gets a stool/crate near the workbench.

These become emotional anchors.

If upset, they go there.

If tired, they go there.

If thinking, they return there.

If the player gives them something sentimental, they may place it nearby.

That creates attachment without building private rooms.

And because they're cute, you can push tiny behaviors very far:

sleeping at the table,
falling asleep reading,
carrying an object that's slightly too large,
struggling to open a jar,
sitting on the floor,
following each other,
getting distracted,
watching rain,
making tea,
stealing each other's tools,
watering a plant twice,
turning lights off behind one another.

These behaviors will sell the game much more than another map.

The room can evolve through five broad visual eras without changing shape:

Era 1: Bare room

Workbench.
Few books.
One plant.
Kettle.
Table.

Era 2: Lived-in

More books.
Food.
Tools.
Notes.
Small inventions.

Era 3: Mechanized

Wires.
Sensors.
Automation.
Computer.
Small robots.

Era 4: Intelligent

Objects communicate.
AI terminal.
Adaptive lighting.
Automated plants.
Fabrication.

Era 5: Almost magical

The room barely looks futuristic in a conventional sci-fi sense.

Instead, everything just quietly works.

Books find themselves.

Lights follow activity.

Plants maintain themselves.

Machines fabricate parts.

The room responds.

That subtlety matters.

Do not make it look like:

CYBERPUNK AI ROOM.

Make it look like the same cozy room that has been continuously improved by two obsessive little people.

That is much more charming and much more aligned with the ideology.

My strongest recommendation would be:

Make the entire playable world one 14×10-ish tile room with a window, central table, workbench, bookshelf/computer corner, plant corner, and kitchen corner.

Then commit to a brutal production constraint:

You are not allowed to add another room until you have created at least 100 meaningful interactions inside this one.

That constraint would probably improve the game enormously.

The room should become deep enough that the player eventually knows every floorboard.
